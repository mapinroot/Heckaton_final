# ReGround

> **Know where your soil is going before you dig.**

**ReGround helps Auckland construction projects plan where surplus excavated soil and rock will go before excavation starts — then match, verify and route suitable material to nearby projects that need it.**

Instead of:

**Dig → discover surplus → search for somewhere to send it → dispose**

ReGround moves the decision earlier:

**Forecast → Match → Agree → Dig & Verify → Route → Reuse**

---

## At a Glance

| | ReGround |
|---|---|
| **Problem** | Suitable excavated material can be disposed of because reuse is difficult to coordinate before excavation |
| **Users** | Earthworks contractors, developers and projects requiring fill or soil |
| **Solution** | Plan surplus material early, match it with local demand and verify actual batches |
| **Initial market** | Auckland construction and earthworks |
| **Climate benefit** | Less disposal and shorter material transport |
| **2035 scenario** | ~222,666 t/year locally reused and ~1,870 t CO₂-e/year transport emissions avoided* |

\*Scenario result based on explicit assumptions described below — not a measured forecast.

# The Problem

Construction projects do not only generate buildings.

They also generate **large amounts of excavated soil and rock**.

Based on the Auckland Council material used in our research, approximately:

# **2 million tonnes of soil/year**

are sent to disposal facilities in Auckland.

Some of this material may be suitable for another construction project.

But reuse is not as simple as finding someone who wants "soil".

A contractor needs to know:

- what material will be excavated,
- how much will be available,
- when it will be available,
- who needs compatible material,
- how far away they are,
- what evidence the receiver requires,
- and what happens if the actual material differs from the plan.

When these questions are answered **after excavation begins**, the project is already under time pressure.

That can lead to:

**Stockpiling → extra handling → longer transport → disposal fees → potentially usable material becoming waste**

---

# How It Is Commonly Done

The industry already has the pieces required to manage excavated material:

- contractors,
- transport operators,
- disposal facilities,
- soil processors,
- laboratories,
- geotechnical consultants,
- environmental consultants,
- and material exchange services.

The issue is that these activities can occur as **separate decisions**.

A simplified reactive process looks like:

```text
EXCAVATE
   ↓
Material becomes surplus
   ↓
Find somewhere to send it
   ↓
Check receiver requirements
   ↓
Arrange testing if required
   ↓
Arrange transport
   ↓
Reuse or Disposal
```

The material itself may not be the only problem.

## The coordination happens too late.

# What ReGround Changes

ReGround does **not** replace laboratories, engineers, contractors or material processors.

It connects them earlier in the construction programme.

```text
BEFORE EXCAVATION

Project plans + reports
          ↓
 Forecast surplus
          ↓
 Find receiver
          ↓
Match material + quantity
+ timing + location
          ↓
Agree acceptance criteria


DURING EXCAVATION

Excavate material
          ↓
Verify actual batch
          ↓
Create digital batch record
          ↓
   PASS / HOLD / FAIL
      ↓      ↓      ↓
    Reuse   Test   Alternative
```

The key change is:

> **The destination is planned before the material becomes an urgent disposal problem.**

# The ReGround Pipeline

## 1 — Project Planning & Forecasting

Before excavation, ReGround builds an expected material profile using information already available to the project.

### Inputs

- Project plans
- Excavation drawings
- Geotechnical reports
- Environmental reports
- Expected material type
- Estimated quantity
- Project location
- Excavation schedule

Example:

```text
Project A
Auckland

Expected surplus: 420 t
Expected material: silty soil
Excavation: 14–18 October
```

The forecast does not claim to know exactly what will be underground.

Its purpose is to give the project enough information to **start planning a destination early**.

---

## 2 — Match a Receiver

ReGround searches for projects that need compatible material.

A match is evaluated using more than location.

### Matching criteria

| Factor | Question |
|---|---|
| **Material** | Does the receiver accept this type of material? |
| **Quantity** | Does available supply match demand? |
| **Timing** | Do excavation and receiving windows overlap? |
| **Location** | Is local transport practical? |
| **Application** | Is it suitable for the intended use? |
| **Evidence** | Are the required reports or test results available? |

Example:

```text
SOURCE

420 t surplus
14–18 Oct
Silty material

        ↓ MATCH ↓

RECEIVER

Needs 300 t
15–20 Oct
Suitable fill accepted
12 km away
```

So ReGround is **not simply a listing marketplace**.

The goal is to identify a **workable source-to-receiver pathway**.

---

## 3 — Agree Acceptance Criteria

Before excavation begins, the receiver defines what must be satisfied before accepting the material.

This may include:

- permitted material type,
- particle-size range,
- contamination limits,
- moisture conditions,
- laboratory results,
- field testing,
- engineering requirements,
- consents,
- and other project-specific approvals.

This creates a clear acceptance profile before trucks start moving.

---

## 4 — Excavate & Verify

The forecast tells us what we **expect**.

The verification stage checks what was **actually excavated**.

Our proposed physical verification system can support checks such as:

- particle-size fractions,
- mass / weight,
- moisture,
- visual consistency,
- and obvious material anomalies.

Each batch can then be connected to a digital record.

### Digital batch record

```text
Batch ID
Source project
Location
Material measurements
Photos
Supporting documents
Receiver requirements
Routing status
```

This creates traceability between:

**the original plan → the actual material → the receiver's requirements**

ReGround does not replace formal geotechnical or environmental testing where this is required.

---

## 5 — Decide & Route

The material receives one of three outcomes.

| Status | Meaning | Action |
|---|---|---|
| **PASS** | Meets agreed requirements | Deliver to matched receiver |
| **HOLD** | More evidence is required | Further testing or professional review |
| **FAIL** | Does not meet receiver requirements | Find another destination or disposal pathway |

This means a mismatch does not automatically break the whole material-management process.

There is a defined next step.

---

## 6 — Deliver & Reuse

A verified batch is transported to the receiving project and used for an agreed purpose such as:

- bulk fill,
- earthworks,
- site preparation,
- landscaping,
- or another suitable application.

The objective is simple:

> **Use suitable material closer to where it was generated instead of automatically transporting it to disposal.**

---

# Why This Is More Than a Soil Marketplace

A marketplace answers:

> **"Who has soil and who wants soil?"**

ReGround tries to answer:

> **"Can this specific material, from this project, at this time, meet the requirements of this receiving project — and can we verify that before delivery?"**

That requires four connected layers:

### 1. Planning
Predict the surplus before excavation.

### 2. Matching
Connect supply with compatible demand.

### 3. Verification
Compare the actual excavated material with the agreed profile.

### 4. Routing
Give each batch a clear PASS / HOLD / FAIL pathway.

That integration is ReGround's proposed point of difference.

# Example Journey

Consider two Auckland projects.

### Project A — Supplier

- 420 tonnes of surplus material
- Excavation: 14–18 October
- Existing geotechnical information available

### Project B — Receiver

- Requires 300 tonnes of suitable fill
- Material needed: 15–20 October
- Located 12 km away

### Without ReGround

Project A may excavate first and then begin searching for a destination.

If no suitable receiver is identified quickly, the material may need to be stockpiled or sent to disposal.

### With ReGround

```text
420 t forecast
      ↓
Receiver found before excavation
      ↓
300 t potential match
      ↓
Requirements agreed
      ↓
Material excavated
      ↓
Actual batch verified
      ↓
PASS
      ↓
300 t delivered for local reuse
```

The remaining material can then be matched separately or follow another approved route.

# 2035 Impact Scenario

We built a simple model to test what ReGround's potential impact could look like at scale.

## This is a scenario — not a forecast.

The model asks:

> **If Auckland's excavated-soil disposal stream grows and ReGround gradually redirects part of it to local reuse, how much transport could potentially be avoided?**

---

## Model Assumptions

| Input | Assumption |
|---|---:|
| Base year | 2026 |
| Auckland baseline material stream | **2,000,000 t/year** |
| Annual material growth | **1.2%** |
| ReGround adoption in 2026 | **1%** |
| ReGround adoption in 2035 | **10%** |
| Example disposal route | **92 km** |
| Assumed local reuse route | **12 km** |
| Heavy-freight emissions factor | **0.105 kg CO₂-e/t-km** |

The analysis begins with 2 million tonnes and applies 1.2% compound annual growth:

```text
Future material
=
2,000,000 × (1.012)^years
```

By 2035:

```text
2,000,000 × (1.012)^9
≈ 2,226,664 tonnes/year
```

Our model then assumes ReGround gradually increases from **1% adoption in 2026 to 10% in 2035**.

Therefore:

```text
2,226,664 × 10%

≈ 222,666 tonnes/year
```

could move through the local-reuse pathway in the scenario.

---

# Why 1.2% Growth?

The 1.2% figure represents a modelling assumption that excavated-material generation increases over time alongside continued:

- population growth,
- construction,
- development,
- and infrastructure activity.

It is **not an official Auckland forecast for excavated-soil growth**.

Its purpose is to avoid the unrealistic assumption that today's 2 million tonne material stream remains completely unchanged through 2035.

---

# Why 10% ReGround Adoption?

We deliberately did not assume that ReGround immediately captures a large share of Auckland material.

The scenario starts at:

**1% in 2026**

and rises gradually to:

**10% in 2035**.

This represents a possible scaling pathway through:

```text
Pilot
 ↓
Validation
 ↓
More suppliers & receivers
 ↓
Network growth
 ↓
10% scenario share
```

The 10% value is an **impact-testing assumption**, not a predicted or guaranteed market share.

---

# Why 92 km vs 12 km?

Our model compares:

```text
BUSINESS AS USUAL

Source
  ↓
92 km example disposal route
  ↓
Disposal
```

with:

```text
REGROUND

Source
  ↓
12 km assumed local route
  ↓
Receiver
```

The **92 km route is a selected long-distance Auckland example**, not the average distance travelled by Auckland soil.

The **12 km distance is an assumed local-reuse distance** used to explore the effect of finding a nearby receiver.

Transport distance is therefore one of the most important assumptions in the model.

---

# How Emissions Were Calculated

Transport emissions are modelled using:

```text
tonnes
×
distance travelled
×
emissions factor
```

### Business as usual

All material follows the modelled disposal route:

```text
Total material
× 92 km
× 0.105 kg CO₂-e/t-km
```

### With ReGround

Only the captured share changes route:

```text
Reused material
× 12 km

+

Remaining material
× 92 km
```

This is important because ReGround does **not** remove transport completely.

It attempts to shorten part of it.

---

# 2035 Scenario Result

Under these assumptions:

| 2035 metric | Result |
|---|---:|
| Projected material stream | **2,226,664 t/year** |
| ReGround scenario share | **10%** |
| Material redirected locally | **222,666 t/year** |
| BAU transport emissions | **~21,510 t CO₂-e/year** |
| ReGround scenario emissions | **~19,639 t CO₂-e/year** |
| Avoided transport emissions | **~1,870 t CO₂-e/year** |
| Reduction | **~8.7%** |

### Why is the reduction 8.7% rather than 10%?

Because the 10% of material redirected by ReGround still needs transport.

It moves **12 km rather than 92 km**.

Therefore ReGround reduces the transport emissions associated with that material rather than eliminating them entirely.

---

# What the Model Does Not Claim

Our scenario is intentionally limited.

It currently does **not** include:

- return trips by empty trucks,
- traffic conditions,
- different truck types,
- loading and unloading,
- screening energy,
- processing,
- laboratory emissions,
- contamination,
- rejected loads,
- actual market adoption,
- or a full lifecycle assessment.

Therefore:

> **1,870 t CO₂-e/year is an illustrative transport scenario, not a measured Auckland-wide emissions saving.**

The next stage is to replace assumptions with actual pilot data.

---

# 15% Circular Material Benchmark

Our analysis also shows a **15% global circular material-use benchmark for 2035**.

This is an external benchmark.

It is **not the ReGround adoption assumption**.

```text
Global circular-material benchmark → 15%

ReGround scenario share → 10%
```

They have different denominators and should not be interpreted as equivalent targets.
